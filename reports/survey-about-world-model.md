# World Models: A Survey of Predictive, Generative, and Embodied Simulators

## TL;DR

World models are learned models that represent how an environment evolves and can be used for prediction, planning, control, or interactive generation. The term now spans several partially overlapping traditions: compact latent dynamics for model-based reinforcement learning, learned models for search and planning, video-generation systems framed as simulators, and embodied/robotic models conditioned on actions or language. Early deep world-model work separated perception, dynamics, and control through a VAE, recurrent stochastic dynamics model, and controller, showing that policies could be trained in learned latent rollouts [1], [2]. Later systems such as Dreamer, DreamerV3, MuZero, TD-MPC2, and DayDreamer show that learned models can support high-performance control through imagined actor-critic learning, tree search, latent MPC, and real-robot online learning [3], [4], [5], [6], [7]. In parallel, systems such as Genie, Sora-style video simulators, iVideoGPT, WorldDreamer, and Pandora push world models toward controllable generative environments and foundation-model-scale video simulation [8], [9], [10], [11], [12]. The central research tension is that visual realism, predictive likelihood, and downstream usefulness are not equivalent: reliable world models must also be action-faithful, uncertainty-aware, temporally stable, physically and causally grounded, and safe under distribution shift [13].

## Background

The phrase “world model” is used at different levels of abstraction. In the modern deep-RL formulation associated with Ha and Schmidhuber, a world model is a learned generative model of an environment: observations are compressed into latent states, a recurrent dynamics model predicts future latent states conditioned on actions and history, and a controller uses those latent features to act [1]. The accompanying project description emphasizes that the model can generate “dream” rollouts in which policies are trained before transfer to the real environment [2]. This formulation made three design commitments explicit: compact representation learning, stochastic temporal prediction, and separation between learned dynamics and policy optimization.

Model-based reinforcement learning is related but not identical. The surveyed MBRL literature defines the field around the use of a known or learned model together with learning of a global policy or value function; it traces the broader planning-and-learning lineage through Dyna-style methods and earlier game-playing and heuristic-search work [14]. Thus a learned world model may be used for model-based RL, but a world model can also be used as a representation learner, a simulator for data generation, or a controllable video generator. Conversely, MBRL can rely on known dynamics or on models that are not intended to generate perceptually realistic observations.

Recent usage has broadened again. Genie is described as a generative interactive environment trained from unlabeled Internet video: it combines video tokenization, autoregressive dynamics, and a latent-action model so that users can interact frame by frame without ground-truth action labels [8]. OpenAI’s Sora research page frames large-scale video generation as learning a general visual simulator, while explicitly noting failures in physical simulation, object-state changes, and temporal/spatial consistency [10]. These examples show why “world model” should be treated as an umbrella term whose precise meaning depends on the representation, prediction target, action interface, and downstream use case.

## Architectures and learning objectives

### Latent state-space models

Latent state-space models remain the dominant architecture for efficient control. Dreamer learns a compact latent dynamics model from images and improves behavior by backpropagating value estimates through imagined trajectories [6]. DreamerV3 extends this line by reporting a single algorithm and configuration across more than 150 tasks, including robot control, Atari, ProcGen, DMLab, and Minecraft [3]. These systems make a practical argument for abstraction: if the latent model preserves information relevant to rewards, values, and controllable dynamics, planning or policy learning can occur without expensive pixel-space simulation.

However, the right abstraction is contested. MuDreamer removes pixel reconstruction pressure from predictive learning and reports improved robustness to visual distractions and faster training, illustrating that reconstruction fidelity can conflict with task-relevant prediction [15]. TD-MPC2 pushes the same principle in a different direction: it performs local trajectory optimization directly in the latent space of a decoder-free world model and reports broad continuous-control results across 104 online RL tasks [4]. The technical tradeoff is therefore not simply “more reconstruction versus less reconstruction”; it is whether the model’s latent variables are sufficient for control, calibrated under uncertainty, and stable under multi-step rollout.

### Search, MPC, and imagined policy learning

World models differ sharply in how they are used at decision time. MuZero learns a model that predicts rewards, values, and policies for tree search rather than reconstructing full observations; it reports strong performance in Atari and superhuman-level play in Go, chess, and shogi without being given the game rules [5]. Dreamer uses imagined trajectories to train actor and value functions [6]. TD-MPC2 uses model-predictive control over latent action sequences [4]. These approaches expose a key design axis: search can improve local decision quality but may be computationally expensive; actor-critic imagination amortizes planning into a policy; MPC can be robust in continuous control but depends heavily on local model accuracy and optimization horizon.

### Token and autoregressive world models

Autoregressive token models import the training logic of language and video models into interactive prediction. iVideoGPT represents visual observations, actions, and rewards as token sequences and trains a multimodal autoregressive transformer for next-token prediction, supporting video prediction, planning, and reinforcement learning [9]. This formulation is attractive because it unifies many modalities under a single sequence objective, but it also inherits sequential decoding cost and sensitivity to compounding errors. Tokenization choices become central: discrete representations can improve compression and modeling convenience, but may discard visual or physical detail needed for control.

### Diffusion and video-generation world models

Diffusion world models move in the opposite direction by emphasizing high-fidelity multimodal future generation. DIAMOND argues that visual details matter in Atari and replaces discrete-latent dynamics with a diffusion world model [16]. Diffusion World Model applies conditional diffusion to long-horizon model-based RL prediction [17]. These methods can represent multiple plausible futures through sampling, but the iterative denoising process is computationally costly for interactive control. The emerging question is whether diffusion models can be distilled, hybridized, or constrained enough to serve as fast action-conditioned simulators rather than merely high-quality video predictors.

### Memory, uncertainty, and representation learning

Long-horizon modeling requires more than next-step prediction. Recall to Imagine integrates state-space memory mechanisms into world models to improve temporal coherence, long-term memory, and credit assignment [18]. Representation learning work using stochastic frame prediction combines stochastic video generation with masked image modeling and reports gains for video segmentation and robotic manipulation [19]. Across these approaches, uncertainty is not optional: stochastic transitions, mixture distributions, ensembles, diffusion sampling, and confidence estimation all attempt to distinguish multiple plausible futures from unmodeled ignorance. A realistic simulator that is confidently wrong can be worse for planning than a lower-resolution model that knows when it is uncertain.

## World models for control and embodied learning

The strongest empirical case for world models comes from settings where interaction is expensive. Dreamer learns behaviors by latent imagination on visual-control tasks [6], while DreamerV3 reports robustness across a broad suite of domains with one configuration [3]. DayDreamer applies Dreamer-style learning directly online to real robots without simulators, including locomotion, manipulation, and navigation demonstrations [7]. These results support the core promise of world models: they can turn limited real interaction into many imagined updates.

MuZero illustrates a different strength: a learned model can be optimized for planning-relevant quantities rather than for full observation prediction [5]. This is powerful in games with reliable simulators, discrete actions, and clear rewards. The embodied-control setting is harder. Robots and vehicles face partial observability, continuous contact dynamics, distribution shift, and safety constraints. GAIA-1, for example, models video, text, and vehicle actions as discrete tokens to generate controllable future driving scenarios, suggesting use as a scenario-generation and prediction tool rather than proving closed-loop autonomous-driving safety [20].

This distinction matters because world-model errors are amplified by control. If an imagined rollout is slightly wrong, a policy may exploit the model rather than learn a robust real-world behavior. The safety survey identifies hallucination, distribution shift, poor generalization, and misleading prediction errors as central risks for embodied AI, robotics, and autonomous driving [13]. Therefore, world-model evaluation for control should measure not only open-loop prediction quality but also whether downstream behavior improves, whether failures are detected, and whether uncertainty changes the agent’s decisions.

## Generative simulators and foundation world models

Large video models have made world models salient beyond reinforcement learning. Sora’s research page describes a diffusion transformer over spacetime patches in compressed latent space and reports emergent capabilities such as some 3D consistency, object permanence, and simulation of digital worlds, while also documenting failures in basic physics and long-horizon coherence [10]. This dual evidence is instructive: scaling video generation can produce simulator-like regularities, but perceptual plausibility alone does not establish causal physical understanding.

Genie represents a more explicitly interactive direction. It learns latent actions from unlabeled video and enables frame-by-frame control in generated environments [8]. iVideoGPT similarly bridges video modeling and agent learning by tokenizing observations, actions, and rewards [9]. WorldDreamer uses masked-token prediction for unsupervised visual sequence modeling and supports text-to-video and image-to-video generation [11]. Pandora links language and video foundation models by consuming previous video states and natural-language actions during rollout [12]. Together, these systems suggest a convergence between foundation models and world models: language provides semantic action interfaces, video models provide perceptual rollouts, and agent-learning objectives provide pressure for controllability and task relevance.

The open challenge is grounding. A generative video model may learn correlations that look like physics without supporting counterfactual intervention, stable object identity, or reliable action consequences. For robotics and autonomous driving, the model must preserve geometry, contacts, occlusions, rare hazards, and multi-agent interactions. For games and digital environments, it must preserve state variables and rules over long horizons. The more a world model is used as a simulator rather than as a visualization tool, the higher the requirement for calibrated dynamics.

## Evaluation, safety, and methodological pitfalls

World-model evaluation is fragmented because different benchmarks test different claims. Open-loop visual metrics assess whether predicted frames look plausible; closed-loop task metrics assess whether an agent actually performs better; safety metrics ask whether the model avoids or detects dangerous failures. The safety perspective paper stresses that current world models fall short of minimum safety requirements for safety-critical embodied agents and identifies guardrails, quantitative uncertainty assessment, trustworthy benchmarks, and mechanistic explainability as important safety challenges [13].

Several methodological pitfalls recur. First, open-loop prediction can overstate usefulness: a model may produce plausible video while ignoring actions or violating causal dependencies. Second, closed-loop data collection does not automatically imply functional utility if the measured outcome is still perceptual quality rather than task success. Third, long-horizon rollout errors accumulate, producing visual drift, state drift, and exploitable reward errors. Fourth, action conditioning must be tested at the level of fine-grained consequences, not just broad prompt adherence. Finally, performance claims across domains are rarely comparable because tasks, reward scales, hardware, simulators, compute budgets, and evaluation protocols differ.

Safety concerns are not limited to physical robots. World models inside agentic systems can mispredict the effects of tool use, web actions, or code execution. The same structural risk appears across domains: when a planning system trusts an inaccurate learned simulator, it may select actions that look good inside the model and fail outside it. The remedy is not one metric but an evaluation stack: calibrated uncertainty, out-of-distribution detection, adversarial testing, causal probes, closed-loop task evaluation, and human-interpretable diagnostics.

## Trends and open problems

A first trend is the merger of model-based RL with foundation-model-scale generative modeling. Latent imagination systems emphasize control efficiency; video foundation models emphasize scalable perceptual generation; newer interactive simulators attempt to combine both. The field is moving from next-frame prediction toward action-conditioned, language-conditioned, and multi-modal world simulation [8], [9], [10], [12].

A second trend is architectural pluralism. RSSM-style latent dynamics, tree-search models, MPC-oriented latent models, autoregressive token transformers, diffusion video models, and hybrid language-video systems all remain active. Their tradeoffs are clear: latent models are efficient but may omit details; diffusion models are expressive but slow; token models are scalable but sequential and compression-sensitive; search improves decision quality but adds computation; policy imagination amortizes planning but can internalize model bias [3], [4], [9], [16], [5], [6].

A third trend is growing emphasis on evaluation beyond realism. The field increasingly recognizes that visual quality is only one dimension. Useful world models need control fidelity, memory, physical consistency, causal validity, uncertainty calibration, downstream utility, and robustness to distribution shift [13].

Major open problems remain. The first is long-horizon compounding error: small prediction mistakes can corrupt both generated worlds and learned policies. The second is action faithfulness: models must respond correctly and consistently to interventions, not merely generate plausible continuations. The third is physical and causal grounding: object permanence, contacts, collisions, conservation-like constraints, and counterfactual reasoning are still fragile in many systems. The fourth is safe deployment: embodied agents require uncertainty estimates, guardrails, and failure recovery before learned simulators can be trusted in high-stakes settings. The fifth is benchmark comparability: without standardized protocols that separate perception, dynamics, control, and safety claims, progress will remain difficult to interpret.

The most defensible conclusion is therefore balanced. World models are already useful scientific and engineering tools for efficient control, planning, data generation, and interactive simulation. Yet the strongest evidence is domain-specific, and the gap between visually convincing generation and reliable, safe, causal simulation remains the central challenge for the next generation of world-model research.
